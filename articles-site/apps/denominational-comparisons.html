      Methodology, rubrics and sources

      How the scores on the comparison page were made, what each number means, and where the information comes from.

      GroupsOfficial-teaching scoresSurvey dataStatisticsLimitationsRubricsSources

      Groups

      The page compares church traditions and movements. Most broad traditions are split into the sub-groups that hold clearly different positions, for example mainline and confessional Lutherans, or the Disciples of Christ and the Churches of Christ, because averaging opposed positions into one score hides real relationships.

      Each group is marked by its relationship to historic Christianity:

      • Inside: churches that most other Christian bodies recognise as Christian, including their baptisms ( groups).
      • Edge: bodies that share most Christian practice but depart from a defining doctrine, such as the Trinity, so that recognition is disputed ( groups: Oneness Pentecostals and Community of Christ).
      • Outside: religions with Christian roots whose baptisms most other Christians do not recognise, or that do not recognise most other Christians' baptisms ( groups). They are shown hollow and can be excluded with the toggles.

      The families used for colour (Catholic; Orthodox and Eastern; Protestant; believers' church; holiness, Pentecostal and charismatic; restorationist in method) follow the companion family-tree map, Models of the Christian family.

      Official-teaching scores

      Each group is placed from 0 to 100 on 16 spectrums. A score describes the group's official teaching or characteristic position, not the average belief of its members. Where a tradition is internally diverse, the score reflects its dominant or officially stated position.

      Placements were made by applying the rubrics below to each body's confessions, catechisms, statements of faith and governing documents, checked against standard reference works on denominations. The five spectrums of church and authority began as placements on the companion spectrum maps and were then re-scored against the same rubrics.

      The scores are ordered estimates, not measurements. A difference of less than about 10–15 points between two groups should be treated as soft.

      Survey data

      The congregant surveys view replaces official teaching with what members of each church say they believe, using Pew Research Center surveys of U.S. adults. Only four spectrums have survey questions published by denomination, and only U.S. churches large enough to sample are included.

      Statistics

      • Correlation: the main figure is Pearson's r between two spectrums across the groups shown. Spearman's ρ, which uses only the rank order, is reported beside it as a check that a few extreme groups are not driving the result.
      • Strength words: |r| below 0.2 is reported as no clear relationship, 0.2–0.4 weak, 0.4–0.6 moderate, 0.6–0.8 strong, and 0.8 or more very strong.
      • Missing data: in the survey view, each correlation uses only the groups with data on both spectrums; the number of groups is shown with each result.
      • Every group counts once, whatever its size: the Catholic Church and a small fellowship carry equal weight.
      • Clusters: spectrums are grouped by average-linkage hierarchical clustering on the distance 1 − |r|, so two spectrums that move together in either direction count as close. A cluster is a set of spectrums whose average |r| with each other is at least the chosen cutoff (0.55 unless changed).
      • Line view: groups are ordered by their score on the first spectrum; the dashed steps are the average score on the second spectrum within each third of the first (0–33, 34–66, 67–100).

      Limitations

      • Shared judgment: the official-teaching scores were set by applying one coding scheme. A consistent sense of which traditions are "conservative" or "liberal" can make spectrums agree with each other more than independent measures would, so correlations among them may be somewhat inflated. Correlations that also appear in the survey data are the most trustworthy.
      • How groups are drawn matters: splitting a tradition into several sub-groups gives it more weight. Results that change sharply when groups are excluded should be read cautiously.
      • Survey coverage: the survey data cover only four spectrums, only the United States, and only 2007–2014.
      • Composite spectrums: some spectrums combine related questions. Exclusivist ↔ universalist mixes whether one church alone holds the truth with how widely salvation reaches; high ↔ low church mixes liturgy, sacraments and clerical order; magisterium ↔ revelation places both strong teaching offices and living prophets near the same end.
